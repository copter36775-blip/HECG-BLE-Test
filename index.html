How this works4

Which beats count. HRV is defined on NN intervals — normal-to-normal — not on every RR the detector emits. An ectopic beat or a missed one puts a spike in the series that is real in the data and meaningless as heart-rate variability, and RMSSD in particular is dominated by exactly those adjacent differences. Intervals outside 300–2000 ms, or differing from the running median by more than 20 %, are dropped and counted, so Beats accepted says how much of the record survived rather than hiding the rejection.

Poincaré. Each RR plotted against the next. The cloud’s width across the identity line is SD1 = short-term variability, algebraically RMSSD/√2; its length along the line is SD2, the long-term spread. A cigar shape is normal sinus rhythm; a fan opening to the right is increasing variability with slower rate; islands off the diagonal are ectopy. It shows the shape of the variability that the three time-domain numbers can only average.

Why Lomb–Scargle and not an FFT. RR intervals are not evenly sampled — each one is a different length, which is the whole point of the measurement. The usual shortcut is to interpolate onto a 4 Hz grid and FFT that, and the interpolation invents high-frequency content that was never measured, biasing HF power upward exactly where the respiratory peak is read. The Lomb–Scargle periodogram is a least-squares fit of sinusoids to unevenly spaced samples and needs no interpolation at all, so nothing is invented before the number is taken.

Reading the bands. HF (0.15–0.40 Hz) tracks respiratory sinus arrhythmia and is largely parasympathetic; LF (0.04–0.15 Hz) mixes both branches with baroreflex activity; VLF needs far longer records than a short strip to mean anything. LF/HF is often called sympathovagal balance and that reading is disputed — report it, do not lean on it. Per the Task Force standard, HF wants at least 1 minute, LF at least 2, and VLF a full 5; the panel says which bands the strip is actually long enough for instead of printing four numbers as if they were equally earned.

How this works1

The percentage is a voltage lookup on a LiPo discharge curve, not a coulomb counter. Its accuracy is set by the SAADC internal reference (±2–3% untrimmed) and two 1% divider resistors — together up to ±150 mV at 3.7 V, which is 20 points of charge in the flat middle of the curve. While charging, the terminal voltage is lifted by the charge current and the percentage is not trustworthy — the tag says so.

How this works4

You are right about the shape: a LiPo does not fall evenly. It drops through 4.20–4.10 V in minutes, then spends most of its life crossing 3.95–3.75 V, then falls off a cliff below 3.5 V. That is why percent cannot be a straight line from volts, and why this console carries a 21-point table instead of a formula.

But that table is a generic LiPo curve — a fair average of cells in general, and not this cell. This pack has already proved it is not average: the one labelled 300 mAh held 55. The fix is to measure this pack’s own curve, which is what a fuel-gauge vendor does and what the board can now do for itself.

Method. Discharge from full to cutoff at the operating current, logging volts against time. The load is constant, so charge consumed is proportional to elapsed time and state of charge at sample i of n is 1 − i/(n−1). Invert that and you have volts → percent for this pack under this load, with no datasheet in it. Noise is removed by isotonic regression rather than a running minimum, which would force the curve monotone by biasing every point down into the noise.

The log lives on the board — one point every 6 minutes for 48 hours — because a 47-hour characterisation cannot depend on a browser tab surviving two days. Charging clears it: the derivation is only true across one uninterrupted discharge, so a run with a seam in it is thrown away rather than quietly averaged. A run that did not start above 4.150 V and end below 3.450 V is marked PARTIAL — export works, ADOPT refuses, because i/(n−1) would then be a fraction of the logged span rather than of the pack.

How this works8

How fast an answer can arrive is set by physics, not by this button: the error on a fitted slope falls as T-1.5, so precision costs time and nothing can buy it back. What GO LIVE does is stop wasting that time — it samples the ADC as fast as it will go, discards nothing, and shows the convergence while it happens.

The headline is fitted over the whole discharge so far, so its interval keeps shrinking for as long as the measurement runs. The four trailing windows answer a different question — what the board is drawing now — and stop improving once they are full.

A number is always shown, with a 95 % interval, from the sixth reading onwards. “Not good enough yet” is the verdict badge’s job, not a reason to show a blank: 4 ± 12 mA after five minutes is a true statement that tells you to keep waiting, and a blank tile tells you nothing at all.

Every assumption behind the number is listed with what it actually measured, so each one can be answered rather than assumed. EXPORT REPORT writes the method, the result, the instrument settings, all eight checks and every raw reading to a text file — a measurement nobody can reconstruct is an anecdote.

On this pack is percent divided by percent-per-hour, so the capacity cancels: it stays right even if the mAh on the label is a lie. Full charge would last and the milliamp figure do not — they are only as good as the number in the Pack capacity box.

A cell straight off the charger falls on its own for a while, which reads as current that is not there. The board reports how long ago the charge current stopped, so this works even when the console was opened after the unplug — the tile says PACK IS RELAXING and counts the wait down.

Independently of that, every window is split in half and the two slopes compared. A constant load discharges a cell along a straight line in charge; relaxation is steep at first and flattens. When the early half is significantly faster the tile reads NOT A LOAD and refuses to call it settled — which matters, because while a pack relaxes every window measures the same wrong thing and they agree with each other beautifully. Two windows agreeing is necessary, not sufficient.

Above about 4.06 V the discharge curve is at its steepest and least like any particular cell, so a few millivolts of error becomes a large error in percent. The reading is tinted and labelled top of curve there. Measure in the flat middle (3.7–3.9 V) for the number you act on.

How this works3

This is the number to trust. An absolute draw carries every systematic error at once — a generic discharge curve that is not your cell’s, a pack capacity that is whatever the label claimed, and a divider that may never have been calibrated. Between them that is easily 30–50 %, and it is not noise: it is the ruler being the wrong length, so no amount of averaging touches it.

A difference cancels nearly all of it. Run a segment, change exactly one thing, run another, and subtract — the curve, the capacity and the divider are common to both and divide out. That is how to answer “what does the microSD module actually cost me” to within a fraction of a milliamp, using a board with no shunt on it.

Both segments must sit in a similar part of the curve or the errors stop being common; the comparison line checks the voltage bands overlap and says so when they do not. Run on battery, off the charger, and do not touch the board while a segment is running.

How this works4

Green is time the core spent in sd_app_evt_wait() instead of spinning. Turn the toggle off and watch it fall — that difference is the saving, measured on this board rather than estimated. Only the acquisition ISR is instrumented, so while a BLE link is up this is an upper bound: the SoftDevice's own radio interrupts also run while the core is nominally asleep.

Wake-ups is how often the core left sleep; full loop passes is how often that wake actually ran the whole loop body. The gap between them is the idle slice doing its job. At 500 SPS a 37-sample block completes only 13.5 times a second, so 36 wakes out of every 37 have nothing to do and should cost one comparison rather than one loop.

Acquisition load is the ADS1292R ISR, and it is set almost entirely by the ADS SCLK: nine bytes at 250 kHz takes 288 µs per conversion, which is 14% of the core at 500 SPS. Run the link test on the Registers tab and raise the clock to whatever this wiring proves it will hold — it is the largest remaining CPU cost on the board.

The buck regulator is the single biggest saving on this list — roughly a third of the core and radio current at 3.7–4.2 V. It is armed rather than asserted: if the board does not survive four seconds with it engaged, the next boot refuses to try again and this tile reads LOCKED OUT. The board stays reachable either way by double-tapping RESET into the bootloader.

How this works4

The XIAO’s RGB LED is common-anode through about 1 kΩ a leg, so a lit channel costs roughly 1–2 mA and white costs three times that. That is the same order as everything else on the board put together once the core is allowed to sleep, which is why this is a power control and not a cosmetic one. On a 300 mAh pack a permanently lit LED is a double-digit share of the budget.

ALL OFF is exactly that — no state colour, no recording blink, nothing. HEARTBEAT is the default: dark except for a short flash of the state colour, red while recording, so the board still proves it is alive for well under a tenth of a milliamp on average. STEADY STATUS is the original V2.0 behaviour.

Brightness between 1 and 99 % binds the three LED pins to a PWM block, and that block holds HFCLK on for as long as it runs — a few hundred microamps whether or not the LED is bright. 0 % and 100 % are pure GPIO and cost nothing to hold. If the goal is battery life, dimming is the wrong lever and OFF or HEARTBEAT is the right one; the tile above says which mode is live.

A colour hold releases itself after 10 seconds, so a board can never be left with its LED stuck on by a console that was closed mid-test.

How this works4

Neither number is reported by the board: SD:BYTES counts committed plus buffered, so it climbs smoothly and a flush leaves no mark in it, while PWR:SDOPS counts the flushes. Every flush empties the buffer — burst full, partial-flush timer and the 30 s FAT sync all go through the same call — so the fill is the bytes accumulated since SDOPS last moved, and the moment it moved is the write. Both arrive once a second, so a fresh write can be up to a second old before it shows here.

The trace above is one bar per write, height = how long the card was busy. It is the measurable half of the current-vs-time capture a sense resistor and a scope would give: at a fixed supply and a fixed SPI clock the card’s write current is roughly constant, so busy time is proportional to the charge that write cost. What it cannot show is the height of the current spike itself, because this board has no sense resistor — it infers current from pack voltage over hours, which has no time resolution below tens of minutes.

What the shape tells you is the same thing a scope would: a card that writes 32 KiB in a steady 16–21 ms is doing what it is told, and one that periodically stops for 70 ms or more is doing internal block shuffling that you are paying for in battery. Card shuffling is the worst write divided by the median — the single number that separated the Home-Mall card from the Acer one. Durations come from SDMS, which the firmware accumulates in whole milliseconds, so each write is rounded down by up to 1 ms.

The red LED is not this. It is a 25 ms heartbeat on a fixed 4 s timer that turns red while recording, so on a Holter run at ~7.5 s per write the two drift in and out of step and mean different things: the LED says still running, this card says the card just woke up. Making the LED flash on the flush instead is a firmware change, and a worthwhile one — it would put the cadence on the strap where you can see it without a phone.

How this works3

The nRF52 SPIM only generates 125 k / 250 k / 500 k / 1 M / 2 M / 4 M / 8 MHz. A requested 200 kHz is physically 250 kHz. The sweep writes and reads back all twelve registers at each clock and reports the highest error-free setting for this wiring.

This is also the largest CPU cost on the board, and therefore a battery setting as much as a signal one. One nine-byte conversion frame at 250 kHz occupies 288 µs, so at 500 SPS the ADS interrupt alone owns 14% of the core and the core cannot sleep through any of it. At 1 MHz the same frame is 72 µs and that figure falls to 3.6%. Run the link test, apply what it recommends, and watch core asleep on the Power tab move.

The firmware keeps the clock in RAM only — it deliberately writes nothing to flash for a number you may retune several times in a sitting — so it boots at the safe 250 kHz every time. Applying a clock here stores it in this browser and re-sends it on every connect, the same way the battery divider calibration works. FORGET stops that.
